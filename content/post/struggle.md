I'm starting the new year in a particularly rough state, even though my social and emotional life has never been better. Around this time last year, I blazed through exams and a big software project, met my current partner, and --- after half a year together --- decided to move in with her; we found and renovated a new apartment. To top it off, I had the opportunity to start a part-time job in the IT division of an insurance provider, a major step up from my previous jobs.

Still, I'm haunted by the upcoming exams, which will determine the rest of my career path. In this post, which I was encouraged to write by a friend, I want to reflect on my past and present situation.

---

# My Background

Immediately after moving to Germany in the summer of 2016, I think my mental health began to suffer. The house my parents built had no room for me, so I was under pressure to move out. Getting accepted into a German university with a high school diploma instead of the *Abitur[^abi]* or an equivalent from other countries (British A-Levels, the French Baccalauréat, the International Baccalaureate, etc.) was tedious and nerve-racking.

After a long back-and-forth with the admissions office[^case] I was finally admitted to the Electrical Engineering program. It was there, in the preliminary mathematics course, that I first felt out of my depth. Although I was fortunate to have excellent teachers in high school, with "advanced" electives such as calculus, many of the notations and methods seemed foreign to me. My first semester was overwhelming, and I secretly stopped going to class in the second.

I was living alone in a new city. Many of the people I met in the first semester dropped out or switched majors[^dropout]. I was extremely lonely, demotivated, and couldn't admit to friends and family that I was lost. Most of my nights were spent drinking or partying to distract myself from the fact that I didn't know what to do with myself. I think it was at this point that I began dissociating, or something of that kind. I don't remember much of the first four semesters, when I lived by myself.

Meanwhile, things were not looking great on my family's side either. My parents divorced (I can very much understand why); after their own struggle, the majority of my family members moved back to the US.[^family] None of them have studied under circumstances similar to mine.

I think my mental health hit rock bottom at this point, and in some ways, I guess I'm lucky to still be here today.

## Coming to Terms with Myself

It was in the spring of 2018 that I received a final warning from the university. My grades were too low, I was about to be kicked out, and I realized I had no option but to change the direction of my life. I clearly wasn't interested in electrical engineering. It was then that I went to a consultation appointment with a university counselor. I was thankful that they took the time to try to help me, but I was fairly unsatisfied with the initial advice I was given.

Even though I had long given up on day-to-day student life, I really wasn't keen on officially dropping out, which was one of the options presented to me. I tried to explain my situation: that I was keen on actually earning a degree, and that I had spent much of my free time beforehand tinkering with *nix and programming. It was here that a new alternative came to mind:

# Switching Majors to Computer Science

I turned in my forms the same week, and fortunately, switching majors was much less of a hassle than enrolling had been the first time.

The first semesters came and went with mixed feelings. I was *really, really* good at most practical subjects. It felt too good to be true to finally take classes like *Programming I* and *Fundamentals of Operating Systems*. I was ecstatic that I could dive deep into the subjects I had spent so much of my free time on.

There was, however, a downside. Computer science is math-heavy, and at the TU9[^tu9] universities, fields like theoretical computer science make up the bulk of the mandatory curriculum. These were the subjects I didn't immediately understand, the ones that gave me anxiety, the ones I instinctively tried to avoid, despite their (I now admit) importance to a well-rounded CS education. I was convinced I couldn't pass them, but I was determined to try anyway. Lo and behold, the struggle was more with myself than with the material, which was never going to be an "easy A" anyway.

## Managing the Situation

To a greater or lesser extent, I've worked very hard to pass the exams for nearly all mandatory and elective classes while working part-time. I'm really looking forward to writing my bachelor's thesis and working full-time: as boring as it may sound, I've realized that I flourish in office environments, working at a computer all day. I spend my free time coding or doing volunteer tech support, and I am at a point where I can very much monetize those skills.

After eight years of on-again, off-again fights with my mental health,[^health] and countless sacrifices of fun and free time to manage coursework and employment ... I really just strive for a life of modern normalcy: a job, and a family to come home to after it.

# The Catch

I've passed all but two courses: Analysis and Discrete Mathematics. These are, on their own, tough subjects, which pretty obviously "weed out" unqualified or lazy undergrads in their first two semesters and provide solid fundamentals to build on in later courses.

The irony, of course: I'm practically done with my studies. This is computer science, and plenty of other subjects have required fundamental knowledge of calculus, sequences, combinatorics, graph and set theory, et cetera.

I attended most math lectures and tutoring sessions offered by the university (unfortunately, I had to work during some others), but much of what I am learning, I am teaching myself.[^tutor] I feel like I am learning in slow motion, taking multiple hours just to understand and then apply a topic that is brushed over in ten minutes during a lecture. The fundamentals just aren't there for me to hit the ground running the way most other students seem to; even the other strugglers I know managed once they applied themselves.

I feel extremely disadvantaged because I work part-time and lack the *Abitur*-level familiarity that is expected. Analysis in particular has been rough: it combines into a single subject what many other colleges teach as Calculus I--III. To pass, I need to be confident with limits, convergence and divergence theorems, vector calculus and multiple integrals, Lagrange multipliers, unit tangent vectors, et cetera, all within a two-hour exam.

This wouldn't be so bad if our study regulations didn't limit us to three attempts per exam[^attempt] before [forced exmatriculation](https://www.reddit.com/r/germany/comments/b5wqvq/exmatriculation_from_a_german_university_and_then/). My first attempt at Analysis was in the summer of 2022, for which I prepared with the help of a private tutor. After failing, I decided to take a break, focus on my other subjects, and retake the exam after attending all lectures and tutorials a second time.[^offer] I took my best shot again in 2023. Despite working late shifts at a movie theatre, I made sure to spend the early part of the day attending classes and practicing all the material offered. In August, I failed again, just barely below the threshold, which sent me into the current downward spiral.

I have been preparing for two months, trying to understand and practice all the material available to me from the university and from other sources on the internet. But if I fail again on my third attempt, I will have fought the toughest battle of my life so far for nothing. I'm not quite sure how to deal with that.

[^abi]: The highest of several secondary education qualifications in Germany, typically completed after 12 or 13 years of schooling.

[^family]: I won't get into further details, as this is a public post in which I cannot divulge too much information about other people's lives.

[^case]: I was the first German citizen to enroll at the university with a high school diploma, and my case fell into a gray zone.

[^dropout]: Many majors are *Zulassungsfrei,* meaning anyone with qualified certificates may enroll regardless of their school grades. As a result, people abandon their current studies due to lack of interest or compulsory exmatriculation after failing to pass exams.

[^tu9]: The alliance of the nine leading technical universities in Germany.

[^tutor]: I had a tutor for linear algebra, which I passed on my first try. Unfortunately, she was not much help with some analysis topics.

[^attempt]: After the third exam attempt is failed, an oral examination is offered, allowing a student to "scrape by" with the lowest possible grade, should they pass it --- this is something I want to avoid.

[^offer]: While exams are offered every six months, lectures and tutorials are only offered in one semester of the year. In the case of Analysis, that is only during the summer.

[^health]: As I have learned through my partner and friends, some of the problems seem to stem from possibly abusive members of my family, and once I have the time, I am committed to working through these issues with a professional.
